A journal should be able to say, in its first article, what it is for. This is that statement.

## The gap

Work on Vedānta tends to fall into one of two failures.

The first is devotional promotion. It praises, but it does not argue. It quotes verses as settled conclusions rather than as claims that can be examined. It treats disagreement as disrespect. Whatever its sincerity, it produces very little that a careful reader outside the tradition could test, and very little that a careful reader inside the tradition could learn from.

The second is a reductive academic posture that explains the tradition away. Here Vedānta becomes a historical artifact, a political instrument, or a psychological symptom — anything except a body of claims about what is real and how one could know it. This posture keeps the vocabulary of rigor while quietly deciding in advance that the tradition cannot mean what it says.

Between promotion that will not argue and dismissal that will not listen, there is a gap. Ayamatma exists to work in that gap: to take Vedāntic claims seriously enough to state them clearly, source them honestly, and subject them to argument.

## What discipline means here

"Rigor" is easy to announce and hard to keep. This journal understands it as a small set of concrete obligations.

Terms must be defined. Words like *ātman*, *brahman*, *māyā*, and *pramāṇa* carry technical meaning that ordinary English equivalents distort. An article that uses them must say what it means by them, and must not smuggle a translation in as if it were the thing itself.

Sources must be traceable. A quotation is a claim about a text. If a reader cannot find the passage, check the edition, and see the surrounding context, the quotation is decoration, not evidence.

Translation must be honest about its choices. Every rendering of a Sanskrit line is an interpretation. An author may argue for a reading, but must mark it as a reading — distinguishing literal sense from interpretive rendering, and citing the published translation when one is used rather than presenting it as original.

Claim must be separated from speculation. It is legitimate to speculate; it is not legitimate to let speculation wear the clothes of demonstration. The line between "the text says," "the text implies," "the tradition holds," and "I propose" must stay visible.

None of this is unique to Vedānta. It is what any honest inquiry owes its readers. The point of stating it is that this subject, more than most, invites the temptation to skip it.

## Essays and articles

Ayamatma the website already publishes essays: public-facing intellectual writing meant to reach readers who are thinking about these questions without a scholarly apparatus around them. Those essays continue, and they remain essays.

This journal is a different kind of object. A journal article is a formal, citable thing. It carries an abstract, keywords, an article type, a stated review path, a volume and issue and article number, a license, and a citation — and, in time, a registered identifier. Those are not bureaucratic ornaments. They are what let a claim be located, quoted, checked, and held to account years after it was made.

Keeping the two layers distinct is a matter of legitimacy, not vanity. A journal that quietly relabels existing blog posts as "articles" has not built a journal; it has renamed a blog. So the essays are not being converted wholesale. Some may later be reworked into articles, but only deliberately — revised, expanded, given full metadata, and marked openly as adapted from an earlier essay, with the original date shown. Reach and rigor are different goods. This site intends to keep both, and to keep them honest about which is which.

## What this journal does not claim

It is as important to state what is absent as what is present.

At the time of this editorial, Ayamatma has no ISSN. It has no registered DOIs; where a DOI appears, it is an explicit placeholder modeling future practice, not an identifier that resolves. It is not indexed. It has no impact factor. It has completed no peer review beyond what each individual article page discloses.

This article is a case in point. It is a signed editorial by the Editor-in-Chief. It was not peer reviewed, and it is not represented as research. Its authority is exactly the authority of a named person stating a position under his own name — no more.

That kind of disclosure is not a weakness to be grown out of. It is the standard the journal intends to hold as it grows. Work authored by the editor will not pass through his own sole review; it will be handled as a signed editorial or sent to an independent reader, and the article page will say which. Overstating status is the one failure a journal cannot recover from, because it corrupts the very thing — trust in the record — that a journal exists to protect.

## An invitation

Ayamatma is small, and new, and says so. What it offers authors is narrow but real: a place where a careful argument about Vedānta, Hindu philosophy, Sanskrit interpretation, consciousness, or the honest boundary between tradition and modern knowledge will be read on its merits, edited seriously, and published without pretense.

If that is the kind of work you want to do, this journal is for you. If it succeeds, it will be because its articles could be trusted — cited, contested, and built upon — and not because it announced that they could.

That is why Ayamatma exists.

*— Kiran Boggavarapu, Editor-in-Chief*

**Editorial development, August 2026.** Ayamatma now recognizes that disciplined inquiry need not originate exclusively in written prose.
