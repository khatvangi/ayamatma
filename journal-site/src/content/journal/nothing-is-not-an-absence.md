Begin by removing things.

Remove the chair from the room. The chair is absent.

Remove every chair from the world. Chairs are absent.

Remove the room, the earth beneath it, the stars beyond it. Every particle, field, force, body and mind. Remove **space**, so there is nowhere for anything to be. Remove **time**, so there is no earlier moment in which something existed and no later one in which it ceased. Remove laws, possibilities, relations, distinctions, observers, propositions.

Now say it:

**Nothing exists.**

The sentence looks intelligible. And yet nearly everything that makes it intelligible has quietly **survived the destruction it describes**. *Nothing* still ranges over a domain. *Exists* still marks off being from non-being. The sentence still presents itself as **true rather than false** — and truth and falsity are a contrast, and contrast is a structure, and structure is something.

**Language is left standing outside the annihilation, reporting the result.**

So absolute nothingness has not been reached. We have imagined an absence while quietly retaining everything required to imagine it.

That is where every discussion of nothingness begins, and it is where almost all of them stop, and stopping there is a mistake — because the argument, as it stands, **is worthless**, and I am going to show you why before I do anything else with it.

---

## The objection that would destroy this essay, if I let it stand

Here is what an honest opponent says, and he says it immediately.

> *Of course you cannot describe it. **There would be nobody there to describe it.** My thesis predicts exactly that. Your inability to state it from inside a world is not evidence against it — it is **entailed by it**. You have discovered a fact about speakers and announced it as a fact about being.*

**He is right, and the argument as given is dead.**

Because look at what has actually been shown. Not that nothingness cannot **obtain**. Only that we cannot **describe** it — that any attempt to occupy the standpoint destroys the standpoint. And **that is a fact about description**.

And it is worse than a mere gap, because it is **precisely the inference this whole line of inquiry exists to forbid**. From a fact about our categories, nothing follows about the world. From a fact about the ensemble, nothing follows about the element. From a fact about our language, **nothing follows about what reality can be**.

To argue *we cannot say it, therefore it cannot be* is to cast a shadow and then point at it as evidence.

**So the linguistic argument must be abandoned. It proves nothing, and it is the wrong kind of argument.**

What is needed instead is a test that bites on **the world** and not on the speaker. And there is one, and it was built for exactly this, and it has been sitting in the Mīmāṃsā for a very long time.

---

## The instrument: *yogyatā*

Kumārila treats absence as **real**, and gives it its own means of knowing — ***anupalabdhi***, non-apprehension, the sixth *pramāṇa*. And he attaches to it a condition without which the instrument is worthless.

The condition is ***yogyatā***: **fitness to appear.**

You may assert that the pot is absent from the room **only because the pot is the sort of thing that would have been apprehended had it been there.** Strike out the fitness condition and the instrument dies on the spot — because then any man could announce the absence of anything whatever, in any room, and never once be wrong.

**Yogyatā is not a condition on what can be said. It is a condition on what makes a negative fact a fact at all.**

That is the difference, and it is the whole difference. It gives us a question we can put to any claim of absence, and the question is not *can you describe it?* but:

> **What, exactly, was fit to appear — and failed to?**

Now put that question to absolute nothingness.

**What would have to be *fit to appear*, and fail to appear, for "nothing obtains" to be a determinate claim?**

Answer: **nothing whatever.**

There is no *pratiyogin* — no counterpositive whose non-arrival the claim reports. There is no *anuyogin* — no locus in which the non-arrival occurs. There is no fitness, because fitness requires a field in which appearing would have been possible, and the field has been abolished along with everything else.

So "nothing obtains" is **not false**.

**It is not a claim.**

And notice what has just happened, because it is the point of the whole exercise. This is **not** the observation that we cannot describe nothingness. It is the observation that **nothingness supplies no conditions under which any assertion about it could be true or false** — and truth-and-falsity are a contrast, and it has abolished contrast.

The nihilist's reply now has nothing to bite on. He said: *of course there would be nobody to describe it.* Quite so. **And there would also be nothing that was fit to appear and did not.** His thesis is not one that we are badly placed to verify. **It is one that has removed, in advance, every condition under which anything could count as verifying or refuting it — including his own statement of it.**

An unfalsifiable claim about the world is a **shadow**. And this is the terminal one.

## Three registers of absence

The architecture can now be laid out.

**Projected absence.** A word arrives with a shadow. *Peace* calls forth *violence*; *purity* calls forth *impurity*; *beauty* calls forth *ugliness*. The opposite may **later** turn out to correspond to something. But it entered first through the architecture of the category, not through the world. **Language manufactured an excluded pole before the world had established that the pole was there.**

**Grounded absence.** Something determinate was **fit to appear** in a specific locus, at a specific time, within a specific process — **and did not**. The pot is absent from the floor. The medicine was not given. The channel was not cut, and the water came.

Grounded absence is not free-floating. It has a **counterpositive** (*pratiyogin*), a **locus** (*anuyogin*), and **fitness** (*yogyatā*). Had the thing been there, it **would have been** apprehended, or **would have** operated.

**Absolute absence** presents itself as a third species: not the absence of one thing from one place, but the absence of **everything**, without remainder.

**It is not a third species. It is the point at which the genus collapses.**

Projected absence is **generated by cognition**.
Grounded absence is **established in the world**.
And absolute absence is **the removal of every condition under which anything could count as absent at all**.

**Absolute nothingness is not the largest absence. It is the abolition of absence.**

---

## Every absence is the absence of *something*

An intelligible absence always has a counterpositive.

The cup is absent from the table. The sound is absent from the recording. The result is absent from the experiment. The act is absent from the sequence in which it was due.

The absent thing need not exist elsewhere. It need only be **determinate enough for its non-presence to mean something**. Without a counterpositive we do not have an absence; we have the *word* "absence," with nothing under it.

Absolute nothingness sets out to remove **every** counterpositive. Nothing remains whose absence could be asserted.

**And then it says that everything is absent.**

There is the concealed contradiction. *Everything* has to remain intelligible **long enough to serve as what was removed**. The concept **borrows the totality it has abolished** in order to describe the abolition. And once the borrowing is refused, nothing is absent — because there is no longer anything of which absence could be predicated.

## And every absence is absence *somewhere*

The locus need not be a place. A note can be absent from a melody, a conclusion from an argument, a reaction from a set of conditions. But **some field of possible occurrence must remain**.

Imagine nothingness as a black expanse, and you have kept **space**. Imagine it as eternal silence, and you have kept **time** and the possibility of sound. Imagine a vacuum, and you have kept a physical domain with describable properties. Imagine an empty universe, and you have kept **the universe**, as a domain that happens to contain nothing.

These are genuine emptinesses. **None is absolute nothingness.**

**You cannot remove the vessel and retain its vacancy.**

Now the honesty this costs, and I would rather say it than have it said to me. **This much of the argument is analytic.** It follows from what we *mean* by absence — from the concept's requiring a counterpositive and a locus — and the nihilist may shrug and say: *I never claimed nothingness was an absence. I claimed it was nothing. You have refuted something I did not assert.*

**He is entitled to that**, and this section does not touch him.

**The *yogyatā* argument does**, and it is the one doing the work — because it is not about what "absence" means. It is about **what makes any negative fact a fact**, and absolute nothingness has abolished the conditions of factuality itself, along with everything else.

---

## Subtraction never completes

The idea usually arrives by a procedure. There is a chair — remove it. A room — remove it. A universe — remove it. Continue until nothing remains.

The procedure looks straightforward **because every subtraction leaves a background intact**. The chair is removed **from the room**. The room from a wider field. The world from a modal or conceptual field in which its removal can still be considered.

At every stage, **something survives that lets us tell what was removed from what was not**.

**The procedure cannot complete itself.** The last subtraction would have to remove not only the last object, but **the operation of subtraction, the difference between before and after, and the domain in which the result obtains.**

If the operation survives, something survives.
If it does not survive, **there is no result** that could be called nothingness.

Enlarging the range of a quantifier does not abolish quantification. A room with no cup is empty of the cup. A world with no cups is empty of cups. **"Nothing" does not emerge when the last item in the inventory is crossed out, because the inventory, the crossing-out, and the domain of enumeration all remain.**

## Zero and the empty set do not help

**Zero is not nothingness.** It is a determinate object with defined relations, distinct from one and from minus one.

**The empty set is not nothingness.** It is a set with no members, and it has identity, formal properties, and a precise position — it is a subset of every set.

The empty set proves that **a structured system can contain a position with no occupants**.

It does not prove that **structure itself can be absent while emptiness remains**.

Which is the same logic again. **The empty set is meaningful because the formal locus survives the absence of its members.** Zero is meaningful because the numerical order survives the absence of counted things.

**Absolute nothingness removes the order and then tries to keep the zero.**

## Can something arise from nothing? — and what the śruti actually says

This is where the tradition speaks, and where I have to be more careful than I want to be, because the evidence is not on one side.

**Start with the Nāsadīya**, and notice that the oldest text refuses *both* terms:

> नासदासीन्नो सदासीत्तदानीं नासीद्रजो नो व्योमा परो यत् ।
>
> *nāsad āsīn no sad āsīt tadānīṃ nāsīd rajo no vyomā paro yat |*
>
> "**There was neither non-being then, nor being.** There was no air, nor the vault beyond it."
>
> — Ṛgveda 10.129.1

*Nāsat.* *No sat.* **Neither.** The poet will not let either member of the pair stand at the origin — which is precisely what you would expect if **both are shadows cast by the pair itself**, and neither was found.

**Now the Chāndogya, and here the ground shifts under everyone.**

Uddālaka, teaching his son, states the doctrine — **and states his opponent's inside the same sentence**:

> सदेव सोम्येदमग्र आसीदेकमेवाद्वितीयम् । **तद्धैक आहुरसदेवेदमग्र आसीदेकमेवाद्वितीयम् । तस्मादसतः सज्जायत** ॥
>
> *sad eva somyedam agra āsīd ekam evādvitīyam | **tad dhaika āhur asad evedam agra āsīd ekam evādvitīyam | tasmād asataḥ saj jāyata** ||*
>
> "Being alone, dear boy, was this in the beginning — one, without a second. **But some say: non-being alone was this in the beginning, one without a second; and from that non-being, being was born.**"
>
> — Chāndogya Upaniṣad 6.2.1

*Tad dhaika āhuḥ* — **"but some say."** He is quoting a live opponent. And then he destroys him:

> कुतस्तु खलु सोम्यैवं स्यादिति होवाच । **कथमसतः सज्जायेत** । सत्त्वेव सोम्येदमग्र आसीदेकमेवाद्वितीयम् ॥
>
> *kutas tu khalu somyaivaṃ syād iti hovāca | **katham asataḥ saj jāyeta** | sat tv eva somyedam agra āsīd ekam evādvitīyam ||*
>
> "'But how could that be, dear boy?' he said. '**How could being be born from non-being?** No — **being** alone was this in the beginning, one without a second.'"
>
> — Chāndogya Upaniṣad 6.2.2

Good. And this is where every essay on the subject stops, and quotes 6.2.2, and moves on.

**Do not move on. The same Upaniṣad says the opposite.**

> **असदेवेदमग्र आसीत्** । तत्सदासीत् । तत्समभवत् । तदाण्डं निरवर्तत ॥
>
> ***asad evedam agra āsīt** | tat sad āsīt | tat samabhavat | tad āṇḍaṃ niravartata ||*
>
> "**Non-being alone was this in the beginning.** That became being. That developed. That turned into an egg."
>
> — Chāndogya Upaniṣad 3.19.1

**That is not a *pūrvapakṣa*. That is the Chāndogya, in its own voice, three chapters earlier, asserting the very thing Uddālaka demolishes.**

So the śruti contradicts itself, on the central question of this essay, inside a single Upaniṣad. And an argument that quotes 6.2.2 and does not mention 3.19.1 is not doing philosophy. It is doing advocacy.

## And Śaṅkara has already given the answer — in this essay's own vocabulary

He has to reconcile them, and he does, commenting on 3.19.1. **And what he says is the thesis of this essay, three lines long.**

> **असदव्याकृतनामरूपमिदं जगदशेषमग्रे प्रागवस्थायामुत्पत्तेरासीत् — न त्वसदेव** । **कथमसतः सज्जायेत** इत्यसत्कार्यत्वस्य प्रतिषेधात् ।
>
> *asad **avyākṛta-nāma-rūpam** idaṃ jagad aśeṣam agre prāg-avasthāyām utpatter āsīt — **na tv asad eva** | **katham asataḥ saj jāyeta** ity asat-kāryatvasya pratiṣedhāt |*
>
> "***Asat*** here means: **this whole world, with name-and-form unmanifested**, existing in the state prior to origination — **and not that it was absolutely non-existent**. Because 'how could being arise from non-being' **prohibits** the doctrine of production-from-non-being."

And then the decisive sentence, which settles what the word *eva* is doing:

> **न तु सत्त्वाभावमवधारयति** । किं तर्हि? **व्याकृतनामरूपाभावमवधारयति** ।
>
> ***na tu sattvābhāvam avadhārayati** | kiṃ tarhi? **vyākṛta-nāma-rūpābhāvam avadhārayati** |*
>
> "**It does not assert the absence of BEING.** What, then? **It asserts the absence of MANIFESTED NAME-AND-FORM.**"

And he adds the word that finishes it: *asad **iva*** — ***as if*** non-existent.

**Look at what Śaṅkara has done, and look at the instrument he used.**

He has taken the *asat* of 3.19.1 and read it as **a grounded absence**. It has a **counterpositive**: *vyākṛta nāma-rūpa*, manifested name-and-form. It has a **locus**: this world, in the state prior to origination. **It has a *pratiyogin* and it has an *anuyogin*, and therefore it is *abhāva* — and not nothing.**

He did not have to be told the difference between the second register and the third. **He was already working in it.**

And I want to be exact about what this does and does not establish, because it is easy to overclaim here and I am not going to. It does **not** show that every *asat* in Vedic literature means the unmanifest; the word is context-sensitive and it will not be disciplined into a single sense.

What it shows is narrower and much harder to get around:

> **Where the śruti says that being arose from non-being, the tradition's own reading refuses to make that non-being *nothing* — and gives the reason: a non-being that could produce would already be *something*.**

Which is this essay's argument, in Śaṅkara's hand, on a verse this essay would otherwise have had to explain away.

---

## Why a source cannot be nothing

Set the texts down and the point stands on its own.

It is often said that the universe may have arisen **from nothing**. But the "nothing" in such statements almost always contains laws, quantum conditions, fields, probabilities, mathematical structures, or a generative capacity. **Whatever the merits of the physics, that is not absolute nothingness.** It is a highly structured situation lacking familiar objects.

And if absolute nothingness genuinely obtained, then:

**A power to produce would already be something.**
**A law permitting emergence would already be something.**
**A possibility of transition would already be something.**
**An instability, a fluctuation, a tendency — each would already be something.**
**And a temporal interval across which any of it happened would already be something.**

**Absolute nothingness cannot contain the resources required to stop being nothing.**

To say the world *emerged* from it is not an explanation of origination. **It hides the unexplained transition inside the word *emerged*.**

---

## Negation is not annihilation

There is one more road to nothingness and it runs through the *śāstra* itself.

The Upaniṣads approach the ultimate by **denial**. *Neti, neti* — not this, not this (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.3.6; 3.9.26). Not gross, not subtle. Not this, and not that either.

And once every familiar predicate has been withdrawn, the shadow arrives on schedule:

> **Then it must be nothing.**

**It does not follow, and the tradition saw the inference coming and blocked it.** The Brahmasūtras answer the *śūnyavādin* in four words:

> **नाभाव उपलब्धेः** ॥
>
> ***nābhāva upalabdheḥ** ||*
>
> "**Non-being — no. Because there is apprehension.**"
>
> — Brahmasūtra 2.2.27

and, on origination:

> **नासतोऽदृष्टत्वात्** ॥
>
> ***nāsato 'dṛṣṭatvāt** ||*
>
> "**Not from the non-existent — because that is not observed.**"
>
> — Brahmasūtra 2.2.25

Read the reason in the first. ***Upalabdheḥ.*** **Because there is apprehension.** Something is being apprehended, right now, by you, as you read — and a blanket negation of being has to account for that and cannot.

That is the *yogyatā* test, run in reverse. **The *śūnyavādin*'s thesis requires that nothing be fit to appear. And something has appeared.**

And this is the direct application of our first register. What has actually happened, when the mind concludes *therefore nothing is there*, is this:

**A word failed to apply.**
**A concept reached its limit.**
**A predicate was denied.**

And from those, the mind **projected** — and the projection is the shadow of the word *being*, which is *non-being*, arriving on schedule, uninvited, exactly as *violence* arrives behind *peace*.

**But the non-application of a predicate is not the grounded absence of reality.** It may show only that the thing does not belong to the class the predicate names. To deny that Brahman is a visible object, a mental construction, a quality-bearing substance, or an entity standing beside other entities **is not to deny Brahman**. The negations prevent the absolute from being reduced to one more item inside *nāma-rūpa*.

**The word stops. Reality does not stop with it.**

## A note on Bhagavadgītā 2.16, and a boundary I crossed on purpose

> नासतो विद्यते भावो नाभावो विद्यते सतः ।
>
> *nāsato vidyate bhāvo nābhāvo vidyate sataḥ |*
>
> "Of the unreal there is no coming-to-be. Of the real there is no ceasing-to-be."
>
> — Bhagavadgītā 2.16

I have argued elsewhere, at length, that this verse **must not** be used as the axiom governing the demerit of an omitted act — that the *asat* of 2.16 is **the unreal**, in a discussion of the Self and its changing embodiment, and that to slide from it to the *abhāva* of a non-performed rite, because the syllables match, is to argue from a dictionary. **Structure, not lexicon.** I hold to that.

**And I am invoking the verse here, and I owe an account of why that is not the same slide.**

It is not, and the reason is the register. The question *there* was **empirical**: what happens in a moving world when a due act is missing? That is *vyavahāra*, and 2.16 has nothing to say about it. The question **here** is whether *sat* itself can become absent — whether **being** can pass into **non-being**. That is the question 2.16 is *about*. It is the only place in this whole inquiry where the verse is on its own ground.

**The pot can be absent. The act can be absent. The body can be absent.**
**The disappearance of a form is not the disappearance of reality.**

That is what the verse says, and it is what it is for, and I will use it here and nowhere else.

## The terminal shadow of the word

Absolute nothingness can now be placed exactly.

**It is the terminal form of projected absence.**

The mind begins with real, determinate absences. *Not this object. Not in this place. Not at this time. Not under these conditions.* Each has a counterpositive; each has a locus; each was fit to appear and did not.

Then language strips the qualifications away, one at a time. *Not this. Not that. Not anything. Not anywhere. Not ever.*

**And the grammatical operation continues after the ontological conditions have run out.**

The shadow of a particular word is a particular negation. **The shadow of the word *being* is non-being.** It arrives because the word arrived, and for no other reason.

But **absolute non-being can never become a grounded absence**. To ground it, something would have to be **fit to appear** and then **fail to appear**, in a determinate field. Absolute nothingness removes the thing, removes the field, removes the fitness, and removes the difference between appearing and not appearing.

**The test cannot even begin.**

Absolute nothingness is therefore not a condition anyone discovered. **It is generated — by carrying negation past the point at which negation has anything to work on.**

> **Negation is cheap. It is generated.**
> **Absence must be earned.**
> **Absolute nothingness can never be earned.**

---

## What this proves — and what it does not

**This does not prove Brahman**, and the limitation is not a modest disclaimer. It is essential, and everything depends on my meaning it.

Showing that absolute nothingness is incoherent **does not** give a positive description of reality. It does not establish that what exists has any of the characteristics Advaita attributes to Brahman. It does not deliver a single positive predicate.

Logic can show that absolute nothingness is not a possible state alongside other states — because the concept has removed the structure required for **statehood**, for **possibility**, and for **absence**. Logic can show that determinate absences do not accumulate into the disappearance of being.

**Logic cannot turn Brahman into the conclusion of a syllogism.**

And Advaita must not introduce Brahman **as the thing that fills the vacuum left by the collapse of nothingness**. That would be the worst kind of argument — an explanatory plug driven into the hole where a negation broke down. It would make Brahman a *posit*, and Brahman is not a posit. The Upaniṣadic claim arrives by a different road entirely: inquiry into what is **presupposed** in every experience, every doubt, every object, and every act of negation — including this one — and which cannot itself be made into one more object among them.

**This argument closes one false road. It does not complete the journey.**

> **Absolute absence cannot obtain.**
> **And the impossibility of absolute absence tells us nothing whatever about the fullness of what does.**

---

## The completed architecture

**Projected absence** — cognition generates an excluded alternative and mistakes the exclusion for something the world established. *Possible, constant, and deceptive.*

**Grounded absence** — a determinate thing, act or relation was **fit to appear** in a determinate locus, and did not. *Real, and informative.*

**Absolute absence** — every thing, locus, relation, time, contrast and possibility is removed. *At that point absence loses its conditions of application, and so does assertion.*

**The first is cast. The second is found. The third cannot obtain, and — more precisely — was never the kind of thing that could have obtained or failed to.**

---

## Nothing is not an absence

Read the title literally.

**An absence must be the absence of something.** Nothing supplies no counterpositive.

**An absence must obtain somewhere.** Nothing permits no locus.

**An absence must be the failure of something fit to appear.** Nothing permits no fitness.

**An absence must contrast with possible presence.** Nothing permits no contrast — and therefore no truth, and therefore no falsity, and therefore no claim.

Absolute nothingness is not an empty room after the last object has gone. **It is the removal of the room, the objects, the act of removal, the difference between full and empty, and the possibility that either could have obtained.**

Nothing remains to which the word *nothing* could apply.

**The word survives only because the world of the speaker survived with it.**

That is its shadow.

---

The mind can **project** an absence.

The world can **ground** an absence.

**Reality cannot become absent.**

**Nothing is not an absence.**

## Notes

**1.** The *yogyatā* condition on *anupalabdhi* — that the counterpositive must be **fit to be apprehended** — is Kumārila's, and is the instrument on which this essay turns. On the Bhāṭṭa theory of *abhāva* and *anupalabdhi*, see Dan Arnold, "Kumārila," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*; G. P. Bhatt, *Epistemology of the Bhāṭṭa School*; John Taber, "Much Ado about Nothing"; Elisa Freschi, "Abhāvapramāṇa and Error in Kumārila's Commentators."

**2.** An earlier version of this essay rested its whole case on the observation that language survives the annihilation it describes. **That argument does not work, and I have said so in the text rather than quietly deleting it.** It establishes something about description, not about being — and it commits the very inference (from our categories to the world) that *The Shadow of the Word* was written to expose. The *yogyatā* argument replaces it, and does not have that defect, because *yogyatā* is a condition on what makes a **negative fact a fact**, not on what makes a sentence sayable.

**3.** The locus-and-counterpositive argument (the sections "Every absence is the absence of something" and "Every absence is absence somewhere") is **analytic** — it follows from what *absence* means — and I have said so in the text. It does not touch an opponent who declines to call nothingness an absence. Only the *yogyatā* argument reaches him.

**4.** Chāndogya 6.2.1–2 with the *pūrvapakṣa* stated in the text (*tad dhaika āhuḥ*); Chāndogya **3.19.1** asserting *asad evedam agra āsīt* in the Upaniṣad's own voice; and Śaṅkara's reconciliation ad 3.19.1 (*asad avyākṛta-nāma-rūpam… na tv asad eva*; *na tu sattvābhāvam avadhārayati | kiṃ tarhi? vyākṛta-nāma-rūpābhāvam avadhārayati*; *asad iva*). **Any treatment of this question that cites 6.2.2 without 3.19.1 is quoting one side of a contradiction.** Cf. also Taittirīya 2.7, which I have not quoted because I have not verified its text against a manuscript tradition, and which is usually adduced here.

**5.** Ṛgveda 10.129.1, the Nāsadīya. The hymn refuses **both** *sat* and *asat* at the origin, which is what the argument of this essay predicts: both are members of a pair, and a pair is a structure, and the structure is what is in question.

**6.** Brahmasūtra 2.2.25 (*nāsato 'dṛṣṭatvāt*) and 2.2.27 (*nābhāva upalabdheḥ*), in the section refuting the Buddhist schools. The reason given in 2.2.27 — ***upalabdheḥ***, "because there is apprehension" — is the *yogyatā* test run in reverse.

**7.** *Neti neti*: Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.3.6 and 3.9.26.

**8.** An earlier draft of this essay quoted Śaṅkara's Māṇḍūkya-bhāṣya to the effect that the objector says *śūnyam eva tarhi tat* — "then it is simply empty." **I have not been able to verify that text and I have removed it.** The Brahmasūtra passages above make the same point and are verifiable. This is recorded here rather than silently corrected, because a fabricated citation on precisely this question would be a self-refutation of an unusually complete kind.

**9.** On Bhagavadgītā 2.16: *The Ontology of Abhāva* refuses to use this verse to govern the demerit of omission, on the ground that the *asat* of 2.16 is the **unreal** and not the **non-occurrent**, and that the slide between them is lexical. That refusal stands. The verse is invoked **here** because here the question genuinely is whether *sat* can become absent — which is the question the verse is about. The boundary is a boundary of **register**, not of vocabulary.
